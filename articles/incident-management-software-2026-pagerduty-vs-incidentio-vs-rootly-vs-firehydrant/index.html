PagerDuty vs incident.io vs Rootly vs FireHydrant (2026): Pricing & AI

2026-10-11 · AI DevOps & SRE · AI Tool Lab Editorial Team · 📖 33 min read
⚡ TL;DR
Compare PagerDuty, incident.io, Rootly and FireHydrant on 2026 pricing, AI response features, on-call coverage, and break-even economics for engineering teams.

Nearly 40% of organizations reported a major outage caused by human error in the prior three years, and 85% of those incidents involved staff failing to follow procedures or procedures that were flawed, according to Uptime Institute’s 2025 outage analysis. The uncomfortable lesson is that incident response is usually a process failure before it is a tooling failure. A chat-based AI assistant cannot compensate for a missing owner, a stale runbook, or an escalation policy no one has tested.

That is the problem AI incident management software is supposed to narrow: find the right responder sooner, bring evidence into the same working context, and reduce the paperwork after recovery. The market does not offer four versions of the same product. PagerDuty sells a broad reliability and alerting platform; incident.io and Rootly make the response workflow itself the center of gravity; FireHydrant combines response management with Signals on-call. Their AI claims and bills differ just as much as their interfaces.

This comparison uses public product and pricing pages checked on October 11, 2026. It separates what is included in a listed plan from add-ons or sales-led features. That distinction matters: an AI recap included in a plan is not the same thing as an autonomous agent that investigates telemetry and drafts a fix.

What AI incident management software needs to do

Start with the work, not the word “agentic.” During a production incident, a useful system should detect or receive the signal, identify the service and owner, page the right person, preserve a timeline, coordinate decisions, and leave behind a review that can change the next response. AI can assist with summarizing noisy alerts, finding related incidents, drafting updates, assembling a timeline, and suggesting likely causes. The human still decides whether a proposed change is safe.

Those jobs sit across three layers that vendors often bundle together:

  • On-call and alert routing: schedules, escalation rules, notification channels, alert grouping, and acknowledgement tracking.
  • Incident command: a shared timeline, roles, stakeholder updates, runbooks, service context, and post-incident reviews.
  • AI investigation and action: summaries, similar-incident search, root-cause hypotheses, remediation drafts, or controlled actions in connected systems.

A team that already has reliable paging may need only the second and third layers. A team replacing a patchwork of phone trees, spreadsheets, chat commands, and retrospective templates may need all three. Comparing a $21 pager seat with a $45 response-plus-on-call bundle without stating the included work produces a misleading spreadsheet.

PagerDuty vs incident.io pricing: pay for coverage or context?

PagerDuty’s public page now presents two products that should not be collapsed into one price. Incident Management Professional is $21 per user each month with annual billing, and the page lists AIOps, PagerDuty Advance, and runbook automation as separately purchased add-ons. Its newer PD Reliability Platform has a different meter: Starter is $2,800 per year and includes 10 seats, 5,000 Signal Intelligence Events, and AI actions; Essential is $12,000 per year with 20 seats and 40,000 events. That is a platform fee with included capacity, not a per-responder quote. Extra usage is handled through credit packs.

incident.io is easier to estimate if the team already knows who responds. The public page lists Team at $15 per user/month with annual billing, plus $10 per user/month for on-call. Pro is $25 per user/month, with on-call at $20. For incident.io on-call pricing, ask whether the quote uses the Team or Pro bundle and whether every person who can be paged needs a paid seat. Its workflow is built around Slack or Microsoft Teams, and the plan comparison labels incident response AI-native while showing Scribe and an autonomous agent as different capabilities. Confirm which agent functions are included versus an add-on; do not infer that the plan price includes autonomous diagnosis.

So the practical comparison is not “which AI is smarter?” PagerDuty makes sense when the organization wants a larger operational reliability system with signal capacity and add-on modules. incident.io is compelling when a responder-centric team wants to keep command, updates, and review in the collaboration surface it already uses. If your stack already has a mature pager, check the integration and migration cost before switching. A lower invoice can be erased by rebuilding every schedule and alert route.

Rootly vs FireHydrant: bundled response or enterprise AI?

Rootly publishes a modular price: Incident Response is $20 per user/month and On-Call is another $20. The two can be purchased separately, and its pricing FAQ says the prices add together when both are used. Incident Response includes AI-related incident grouping, an AI Scribe, retrospectives, workflows, and status pages. Its AI SRE product is a separate, sales-led tier that requires Incident Response and is the part aimed at root-cause evidence, autonomous agents, and draft remediation steps. The boundary is unusually clear on the pricing page, which helps a buyer model the first phase without assuming the most ambitious agent is included.

FireHydrant’s free plan covers up to 10 responders, two runbooks, a Slack and Teams chatbot, one public status page, and three integrations. Pro is $25 per responder/month billed annually and adds runbooks, service catalog, Signals scheduling, and alerting. The page places FireHydrant AI—summaries, meeting transcripts, triage, retrospectives, and follow-ups—on Enterprise, alongside features such as audit logs, SCIM, and private status pages. That makes its entry bill attractive for a small team, but the AI-inclusive production quote may be materially higher than the Pro number.

For Rootly pricing per user, count the products you will actually activate: response alone is $20; response plus on-call is $40 before any AI SRE quote. For a FireHydrant alternative to PagerDuty, compare who owns alert delivery, not just the incident room. FireHydrant Pro includes on-call Signals; PagerDuty Professional has its own on-call and incident-management package, while more advanced AI/AIOps may add cost. These are different bundles, so “cheaper” only means something after mapping the required features line by line.

A 12-responder cost model—and the caveats

Here is a simple annual baseline for 12 responders, before tax and add-ons: PagerDuty Professional is about $3,024; FireHydrant Pro is $3,600; Rootly Incident Response plus On-Call is $5,760; incident.io Pro plus On-Call is $6,480. The arithmetic is monthly per-user price × 12 responders × 12 months. For incident.io’s Team tier plus on-call at annual rates, the baseline would be $3,600, but it does not include every Pro capability. PagerDuty’s $3,024 baseline also does not buy the separate AIOps or PagerDuty Advance add-ons.

These numbers are not a winner ranking. PagerDuty’s newer Reliability Platform starts at an annual platform fee with included seats and event capacity, while its classic Professional plan is per user. FireHydrant’s AI functions are shown under Enterprise, and Rootly AI SRE is quote-led. The $3,600 and $5,760 comparisons describe public base products, not equivalent AI packages. Use a three-column quote sheet: base response, on-call/alerting, and AI functions. Add usage limits and overages beneath each column instead of hiding them in a footnote.

Check four billing details before comparing quotes. First, is a “responder” any user who can be paged, or only a paid seat? Second, are schedules, phone/SMS notifications, status pages, and integrations included? Third, does the AI feature have a separate usage allowance, and what happens when it runs out? Fourth, can the vendor keep the incident timeline and logs inside your required retention and access-control boundary? A tool that looks cheap until the annual true-up is not a low-cost tool.

Calculate the ROI without believing the demo

Model the value in responder-hours first. Suppose 12 people each recover one hour a month from faster context gathering, fewer duplicate pages, or shorter post-incident write-ups. That is 144 hours per year. At a hypothetical loaded engineering cost of $85 an hour, it represents $12,240 of capacity. This is an opportunity-value estimate, not a guaranteed payroll saving: it becomes cash only if the recovered time reduces overtime, contractor spend, or another budget line.

At that same $85 rate, the public baseline above needs roughly 36 recovered hours annually to equal PagerDuty Professional’s $3,024, 42 hours for FireHydrant Pro, 68 hours for Rootly response plus on-call, and 76 hours for incident.io Pro plus on-call. Add the quote for paid AI, migration labor, notification charges, and training before treating those as break-even targets. Treat incident response platform ROI as recovered responder capacity and less operational interruption, not a vendor’s unsupported percentage. If the vendor cannot show how a feature changes an operational metric, do not put its claimed “hours saved” into the business case.

Use the Uptime Institute findings as a reason to test process execution, not as a promise that a tool prevents outages. The report says nearly 40% of organizations suffered a major human-error outage over a three-year period, and 85% of those incidents involved procedure failures or flawed processes. This is where AI incident management software can have a defensible role: it may reduce coordination toil without weakening the change-control boundary. A platform may make the approved steps easier to find and record whether they happened. It cannot make an unsafe runbook correct. Keep the incident commander in charge, require review for production changes, and audit suggested actions before automating them.

For a 30-day pilot, run the candidate against the same services and incident classes as the incumbent. Teams comparing AI incident response tools for SRE teams should track median time to acknowledge, time to identify the owning service, time from first page to a useful hypothesis, duplicate-page count, number of human handoffs, and time to publish a usable postmortem. Record false correlations and incorrect summaries as defects. Include a “no incident” drill to see whether on-call rotations, test pages, and escalation overrides actually work. A polished demo is not evidence of safe performance at 03:00.

Which tool should you pick?

  • Choose PagerDuty when cross-team alert routing, escalation policy maturity, and broader reliability operations are the priority. Ask for the full quote for AIOps or PagerDuty Advance and model event/credit consumption; do not budget from the $21 seat alone.
  • Choose incident.io when the team coordinates in Slack or Teams and values clear per-user pricing for response plus on-call. Check the annual Team versus Pro capability gap and ask for the exact price and scope of any autonomous agent.
  • Choose Rootly when you want modular $20 response and $20 on-call products, with useful incident summaries included before buying AI SRE. Ask for a demo using a real prior incident, and price the root-cause agent separately.
  • Choose FireHydrant when the free tier or $25 Pro plan covers the runbook and on-call baseline, and Enterprise AI has a measured payoff. Get a written Enterprise quote before making the AI feature the reason to select it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is PagerDuty better than incident.io for on-call?

Neither is universally better. PagerDuty Professional is listed at $21 per user/month with annual billing and targets standardized paging and escalation; incident.io Team starts at $15 annually billed, with on-call at $10 extra per user/month. Compare schedule features, alert sources, notification channels, and the actual AI add-ons in your quote. For a large organization, PagerDuty’s separate Reliability Platform may be a closer comparison than its per-seat plan.

How much does incident management software cost for a 12-person team?

Using current public annual prices, a 12-person baseline ranges from about $3,024 for PagerDuty Professional to $6,480 for incident.io Pro plus on-call. FireHydrant Pro is about $3,600, and Rootly response plus on-call is about $5,760. Those totals omit enterprise AI quotes, some usage charges, taxes, migration, and any seat minimum. They are starting points for a quote comparison, not final contract totals.

Does Rootly include AI SRE in the $20 plan?

No. Rootly lists Incident Response at $20 per user/month and includes AI-related incident features, Scribe, and retrospectives. AI SRE is sales-led, requires Incident Response, and is described as the product for evidence-backed root-cause work and remediation drafts. Ask for its separate price, data sources, approval controls, and usage limits.

Is FireHydrant a good alternative to PagerDuty?

It can be, especially if you want runbooks and Signals on-call in the same product. FireHydrant lists a free tier for up to 10 responders and Pro at $25 per responder/month billed annually; its AI features appear on Enterprise. PagerDuty has a $21 Professional seat price and a separate Reliability Platform with included AI actions and event capacity. Compare alert coverage and the cost of the AI tier, not only the headline price.

The final decision

The best AI incident management software is the system that shortens a verified step in your response without making ownership less clear. For mature alerting, price PagerDuty’s full AI-enabled configuration rather than its base seat. For Slack-centered response, compare incident.io’s Team and Pro totals after on-call. For modular buying, Rootly’s $20 response and $20 paging products make the budget visible, while FireHydrant’s Enterprise gate for AI deserves an early quote. Run the same incident drill in each product, log the failures, and buy only the capability that changes your own response metrics.
