We compared Topaz Video AI, AVCLabs, HitPaw and CapCut as AI video enhancers for upscaling, denoising and restoring old footage — with real pricing, GPU requirements and cost math.

A professional restoration house will charge you $40 per hour of VHS at the cheap end and up to $600 per 15 minutes of 4K restoration at the high end — roughly $1,200 to $2,400 for a single hour of finished footage. Spoiler: the answer depends less on the software and more on your GPU, your footage, and whether you plan to do this once or forever.

What AI video enhancers 2026 actually fix

Before you spend anything, understand the job. AI video enhancers 2026 do four distinct things, and each one is a separate model with separate strengths:

Upscaling. This is the headline feature. The tool takes 480p or 720p source and reconstructs a 1080p or 4K frame by predicting detail that isn't really there. Topaz calls its models Proteus, Iris, Gaia and Theia. AVCLabs uses a transformer-based pipeline. HitPaw has an animation model that handles flat colors and hard lines better than anything else in this list. Every serious AI video upscaler claims 4K output; the difference is how much edge haloing and flicker you get in motion. And if your job is to upscale old video footage shot on tape or an early smartphone, the desktop tools are the only ones worth your time — the online freebies stretch pixels and call it AI.

Denoising and deblurring. Old tape and phone footage carry grain, compression artifacts and motion blur. The denoise pass cleans the grain *before* upscaling — run it after and you're just sharpening noise. This is the single biggest quality lever, and it's why the "AI" in these tools matters. Bicubic scaling can't do this.

Deinterlacing. If your footage came from a camcorder or TV capture, every frame is actually two half-frames scanned at different times. Interlaced video looks like a comb on modern screens. All three desktop tools deinterlace; CapCut mostly doesn't.

Frame interpolation. This creates new frames between existing ones — 24fps footage becomes 60fps, which is what you want for smooth slow motion from standard-rate source. Topaz's Chronos and Apollo models are the reference here; HitPaw and AVCLabs have interpolation models too, but their output is softer on fast motion.

Now the hard truth nobody puts in a feature list: an enhancer cannot invent detail that the source never contained. A 320p YouTube rip that's been re-encoded three times is mostly gone. Upscaling it gives you a bigger, blurrier file. The tools work best on footage with real structure — VHS, MiniDV, 1080p phone footage, AI-generated clips — not on compressed garbage. If you're trying to fix a WhatsApp-forwarded video, stop now. That's not what video restoration software does.

One more thing: these are enhancers, not editors and not generators. If you need to cut, caption, or trim, you want an editor — see our AI video editing tools roundup. If you need to *create* footage from text, that's a different category entirely — AI video generation tools — and enhancers are the right follow-up step for the 720p output those generators produce.

Topaz Video AI — the quality benchmark, if your GPU can take it

Topaz Video AI is the professional standard for upscaling and restoration, and every other product in this category positions itself against it. It runs entirely on your machine — nothing leaves your computer, which matters if the footage is a client's. It upscales to 8K, deinterlaces, denoises, deblurs, stabilizes, and interpolates frames, and its temporal models keep frames consistent so you don't get the flicker that image-based upscalers produce when applied to video.

Topaz Video AI pricing is the most important number here: $299 one-time, which includes a year of model updates. After that, the software keeps working forever; you only pay again ($99/year) if you want newer models. Compare that to the $1,200 to $2,400 an hour a restoration service charges, and the license pays for itself on the first real job. That's not marketing math, that's the price list of actual restoration vendors I checked.

The catch is hardware. Topaz recommends an NVIDIA GPU with at least 4GB of VRAM; an RTX 3060 or better is the practical floor. Apple Silicon Macs work and perform well, but a MacBook Air will crawl on 4K output. If you want an AI video enhancer for Mac with serious power, budget for an M-series Pro or Max chip or an external GPU. Integrated graphics will technically run, but a 10-minute clip can take hours — preview before committing, and don't skip the grain-removal pass.

Who should buy it: anyone with recurring restoration work — wedding editors delivering 4K from 1080p source, archivists, people monetizing old footage libraries. Who shouldn't: someone with one family tape and a laptop without a discrete GPU. You'd be buying a race car for a grocery run. And if your archive is mostly stills instead of motion, Topaz Photo AI is the photo-side counterpart — same company, same approach, one frame at a time.

AVCLabs Video Enhancer AI — the easier rival with the better faces

AVCLabs is the main alternative, and the direct Topaz Video AI vs AVCLabs comparison comes down to workflow versus ceiling. AVCLabs runs on Windows, macOS, and has a cloud version — no GPU required if you use the cloud tier. Its interface is simpler than Topaz's: import, pick a model, export. Its standout is face refinement: in side-by-side tests of a blurry 360p home video, AVCLabs reconstructed facial features that other tools turned into smooth blobs.

Pricing: $39.95/month, $119.95/year (frequently discounted to about $96), or a perpetual license that lists at $299.90 but routinely drops to $149.90 on sale. There's a free version with a watermarked 30-second export limit, so you can test your actual footage before paying. The free trial alone makes it worth installing if you're comparing tools.

The tradeoffs: quality on extreme restoration is a notch below Topaz in most shootouts, especially on film grain and motion. Fast-moving scenes can show edge smoothing artifacts. And the subscription price is steep if you only have one project — the perpetual license on sale is the only plan that makes sense for a one-off.

HitPaw Video Enhancer — for beginners and anime

HitPaw Video Enhancer (now branded VikPea) is the one-click option. Upload, pick a model, preview three seconds, export. It has specialized models — General Denoise, Face, Animation, Colorize, Color Enhancement, Frame Interpolation, Stabilize, Low-light — and its animation model is genuinely good, often beating Topaz on flat-color content like cartoons and game captures. For family archivists colorizing black-and-white home movies, the Colorize model does a respectable job with zero learning curve.

Pricing is the weak point: $42.99/month, $99.99/year, or $349.99 lifetime — the most expensive perpetual license in this comparison, and there's no free export at all, just a 3-second preview. One license covers one PC. HitPaw also can't apply multiple models in one pass; upscaling and colorizing means running the video twice.

Who should buy it: non-technical users, anime fans, and people doing one-off family restorations who won't touch advanced settings. Who shouldn't: anyone processing more than a few clips, because the price and the double-pass workflow add up fast.

CapCut — the free option you already have

CapCut is not a restoration tool. It's a social video editor with light enhancement features — sharpen, denoise, and 4K upscaling presets inside its Magic Studio. But if your goal is "make this clip look less terrible for a Reel," it's genuinely the best value: the free tier exports up to 1080p with watermarks on some premium effects, and CapCut Pro runs about $10 to $20 per month depending on region and billing cycle (pricing was restructured in May 2025, so check capcut.com rather than trusting any third-party list).

The limits are real: no serious deinterlacing, no frame interpolation beyond basic slow-motion, and upscaling quality is a distant third behind the desktop tools. CapCut is the answer to "I need to improve this phone clip for TikTok," not "I need to restore 200 hours of MiniDV." For that heavier job, budget for one of the desktop tools below.

Cost math: what "restore old home videos with AI" really costs

Let me put real numbers on this, because the pricing pages hide the actual economics. A typical 10-minute 1080p home video with moderate noise, sent to a professional service, runs $150 to $400 depending on the vendor — that's the range I found across current restoration price lists, from $5.50/minute basic cleanup to $12.50/minute 4K restoration. VHS-grade transfer and cleanup runs $40 per hour of tape at the budget end. Film archives charge far more.

Now run that same 10-minute clip through Topaz on an RTX 3060: roughly 30 to 60 minutes of render time, some electricity, and if you already own the license, essentially $0 per job. The license amortizes to near-zero after your first full restoration. If you're restoring a wedding archive or a deceased parent's tape collection — 10, 20, 50 hours of footage — the professional route is $5,000 to $50,000. The software route is $299 once, plus your GPU and your time. That's the entire argument for buying one of these tools, and it's why this category exists at all.

The hidden cost is your GPU. If you don't own one with decent VRAM, the cheapest real path is AVCLabs' cloud tier (no hardware needed) or buying a used RTX 3060 before you buy the software. A $200 used GPU plus a $299 license still beats $5,000 of professional restoration on a 50-tape project.

How to enhance low quality video without losing your weekend

If you want to actually upscale video to 4K or fix a blurry clip, the workflow is the same across all three desktop tools:

  • Digitize first. If your source is tape, capture it at the highest quality your deck supports *before* any enhancement. Enhancement can't recover what the capture step threw away.
  • Denoise before you upscale. Grain removal first, then upscaling. The reverse order just sharpens the noise. This is the #1 amateur mistake.
  • Preview on a 10-second slice. Render a short section with your chosen model before committing to a full export. A wrong setting on a 10-minute clip wastes 30 to 90 minutes of GPU time.
  • Match the model to the content. Animation footage wants an animation model. Interlaced camcorder tape wants a deinterlace model first. Faces in low resolution want a face model. The generic "upscale" preset is the worst option for everything.
  • Export at the resolution you'll actually use. 1080p → 4K is where the visible gain lives. 4K → 8K is mostly wasted for YouTube and phones.

That's the whole process. Thirty minutes to learn, then it's batch work — load a folder, apply the same settings, walk away.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Topaz Video AI worth it in 2026?

If you have recurring restoration work, yes — $299 one-time beats $1,200 to $2,400 per hour of professional restoration on the first big job, and the software keeps working after the included update year ends. If you have one tape and no GPU, no. Run the free trials first: Topaz offers a watermarked trial on most releases, and AVCLabs' free version lets you export 30-second watermarked clips to judge the actual result on your footage.

Can AI restore old home videos with AI? I mean, really restore them?

Within limits. AI can remove grain, deinterlace, stabilize shake, sharpen faces, and upscale old home videos with AI to 4K — that's real and impressive on tape or MiniDV source. It cannot bring back detail that was never recorded, and heavily compressed re-encoded files stay mushy no matter what you pay. The degradation ceiling is the source tape's generation, not the software's skill.

What is the best free AI video enhancer?

CapCut's free tier is the most capable zero-cost option for short social clips, with light sharpen, denoise and upscale presets and 1080p export (some watermarks on premium effects). AVCLabs' free version adds a 30-second watermarked export — enough to test your footage before buying. There is no truly free desktop restoration tool that rivals Topaz; treat "free" as a trial, not a product.

Do I need a powerful GPU for video upscaling?

For the desktop tools, yes — that's the real system requirement. Topaz recommends an NVIDIA card with 4GB+ VRAM, and renders can run many times the clip length on weak hardware. Apple Silicon Macs work, and M-series Pro/Max chips handle it well. If you have no GPU, AVCLabs' cloud tier is the only desktop-brand option that runs without local hardware.

Should I use an enhancer or an editor?

Both, in that order. Enhance first (denoise, deinterlace, upscale), then edit (cut, caption, export). Running an editor's built-in upscale on already-degraded footage gives you a bigger version of the same problem. If you're starting from AI-generated clips, upscale them after generation, before you cut — that workflow is why AI video generation tools and enhancers pair so well.

Bottom line

Buy Topaz Video AI if you have serious footage and a real GPU — it's the quality ceiling, and the one-time license is the cheapest per-job cost in this category. Choose AVCLabs if you want an easier workflow, cloud processing, or the best face restoration, and grab the perpetual license on sale. Pick HitPaw only if you're a beginner working mostly with anime or single family clips — the price is the worst here. Start with CapCut free if you're not sure you need any of this.

The broader point: AI video enhancers 2026 have turned a $5,000-per-project professional service into a $299 tool you run on your own machine. Reshooting isn't the only option anymore, and for archival, wedding, or family footage, it was never the right one. Test your worst clip on a watermarked trial before you pay for anything — the tools will tell you quickly whether your footage is worth saving. Most of it is. Some of it isn't. Either way, you'll know for the price of a weekend.
