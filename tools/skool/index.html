What Skool Actually Is (From Someone Running a Paid Group)

I've run a paid Skool community for two years, and before that I burnt months on a Facebook Group, a Discord, and a Patreon that never quite clicked. So this is written from the owner's chair, not Skool's brochure. Skool crams three things into one app: a community feed, a course area they call the Classroom, and a calendar. Members pay you a recurring or one-time fee to get in, and Skool handles the billing through Stripe. The part that hooked me was the points system - members level up by posting and reacting, and a leaderboard sits at the top of the group. It sounds like a sticker chart for adults, but it works: my quiet members started posting because they wanted the next level.

How People Make Money on It

Skool is not a traffic source. It's the place you send people who already trust you. The four ways I've seen creators turn it into income:

  • Coaching and masterminds. Charge $99-497/month for a group where you run weekly calls and answer questions. This is the most common Skool business and the one with the best margins, because the $99 platform fee stays flat no matter how many join.
  • Course plus community. Sell a course, but host it inside Skool and wrap a paid group around it. Completion rates beat a lonely Teachable course because the feed pulls people back.
  • Free group as a funnel. Run one free community (you still pay $99/month to host it) as a lead magnet, then pitch a paid group inside it. Most big Skool operators run exactly this two-layer setup.
  • Affiliate income. Skool pays a 40% recurring commission if you refer other creators. It's one of the better affiliate deals around, and it pairs naturally with a 'how I built my community' content trail.

The Fee Math, Spelled Out

On the Pro plan Skool charges a flat $99/month and takes 0% of what your members pay. Stripe takes its normal ~2.9% + $0.30 per transaction. That's the whole cut. Compare that to Patreon (8-12% plus processing), Whop (up to 30% on its marketplace), or Substack (10% plus Stripe). On a $5,000/month community, Skool saves you roughly $400-600 a month versus Patreon and thousands versus Whop.

Break-even is easy to picture:

  • $10/month price -> about 11 members to cover the fee
  • $35/month -> about 3 members
  • $99/month -> about 1 member
  • $500/month mastermind -> one member pays for the whole platform with room to spare

One caveat on price: a few 2026 reviews mention a cheaper 'Hobby' tier around $9/month that adds a 10% transaction fee. Skool changes plans by region, so check skool.com for what's live where you are before you commit. The 14-day trial needs no credit card to build, which is the rare honest trial in this space. Payouts run through Stripe Connect on a rolling schedule, and your first payout can take up to two weeks to clear.

Where It Falls Short

I'll be straight about the annoyances, because they bit me:

  • No built-in email or automation. Want a welcome DM, a churn alert when someone cancels, or a tagged segment? None of it exists natively. Past ~80 paying members I was doing manual ops until I wired Zapier and ConvertKit on top - more subscriptions, more bills.
  • The course tools are bare. No quizzes, no certificates, no drip scheduling, no completion tracking. If you teach accredited or structured material, Skool will feel thin and you'll want Teachable or Kajabi beside it.
  • One price per community. You can't run Basic/Premium/VIP inside a single group. Three tiers means three $99/month communities. The cost stacks fast if you like to segment.
  • Stripe only, skool.com branding. No PayPal, no ACH. Your group lives on a skool.com address with Skool's logo always showing - you can't white-label it as your own product.
  • $99 hits even when you're not earning. A tiny or free community still pays the full fee. If you're pre-audience, that $99 stings before any member joins.

How I'd Start If I Were You

  • Take the 14-day trial seriously - build the group skeleton, post three lessons, invite three friends to test.
  • Pick one offer and one price. $29-49/month is the sweet spot for a first paid group; don't launch five tiers.
  • Move your most engaged people in first. A live group beats an empty one, so seed it with real members before you open the door.
  • Set up one automated bridge (Zapier -> your email tool) so new members land on your list. Do this on day one, not after you're drowning in DMs.
  • Show up weekly. Skool's whole bet is that a steady host keeps a group active - if you go quiet, the leaderboard goes quiet too.

Skool won't make you money by itself. It just stops the platform from taking a cut while you build the thing that does.

❓ FAQ

How much does Skool take, and what do you keep per member?

On the Pro plan Skool takes 0% of your member payments - you keep the full amount after Stripe's standard processing of about 2.9% plus $0.30 per charge. So a member paying $29/month leaves you about $27.85, minus your $99 flat platform fee split across all members. Some 2026 reviewers also mention a cheaper Hobby tier (~$9/month) that adds a 10% transaction fee; plans vary by region, so confirm the live options at skool.com before you sign up.

Can you actually make money on Skool, or is it hype?

You can, but only if you already have an audience or a clear offer - coaching, a paid mastermind, a course, or a niche community people pay to join. Skool is the plumbing, not the traffic. The break-even math is friendly: at a $35/month price you need about 3 paying members to cover the $99 fee; at $99/month you need roughly one. The creators who fail treat Skool like a magic money button and never drive anyone to it.

Skool vs Patreon or Whop - which should I pick?

Pick Skool if engagement and a course attached to a paid group matter more than raw reach. Patreon is better for a simple fan club and brings some discovery; Whop is a storefront for products and takes up to 30% on its marketplace. Skool's edge is the 0% cut and the gamification that keeps a group alive. If you sell one-off files, Gumroad is cheaper; if you write for a paid newsletter, Substack fits. Most creators I know run Skool for the community and link out to the others.
