Pay $100 per game · keep 70%
Steam Direct charges a one-time $100 fee per app (recoupable as a payout credit once the game passes $1,000 adjusted gross revenue; free games pay it too; demos and DLC attached to the same app need no extra fee, while standalone apps each cost $100). Revenue share starts 70/30 in your favor - 75% to you above $10M lifetime revenue and 80% above $50M, applied incrementally with the tier set by the prior year's earnings. Valve collects and remits sales tax/VAT, processes payments, hosts builds and runs refunds inside that 30%. Payouts go monthly by bank transfer roughly 30 days after month end, with a $100 minimum balance and local currency where supported. Non-US developers face up to 30% US tax withholding unless the W-8BEN/W-8BEN-E treaty form is filed in Steamworks before the first payout (most countries drop to 0-10%). No monthly fees, no renewal fees, no per-download charges. The real pre-launch cost is mandatory waiting: 30 days after fee payment for identity verification, a Coming Soon page live for at least 2 weeks, plus store-page and build reviews (1-5 business days normally, up to 12 during the 2026 submission surge).

What Steam Direct Actually Is

I paid the $100 fee in March 2026, uploaded a build, and my first game was live on Steam about six weeks later. Nobody at Valve judged whether it was good. Two reviewers checked that the store page was honest and the build ran on a clean machine, and that was the entire ceremony. That is Steam Direct: Valve's self-publishing door since it replaced Greenlight in 2017. No publisher, no pitch deck, no quality gate. You pay $100 per app, pass a compliance review, and your game sits on the largest PC game store in the world - Valve's own 2024 reporting put it at 132 million monthly active users.

Since January 2024, Steam is also the only major game store that publishes AI use. When generative AI touched what players see or hear, the store page carries an AI Generated Content Disclosure. Epic, PlayStation, Xbox and Nintendo show nothing comparable. If you build with AI tools - and on this site, odds are you do - that disclosure section is part of your business plan whether you like it or not.

The Money Angle

Ship the game AI made possible. The old budget wall - $5,000-15,000 for art, audio and localization on a small game - is mostly gone. Coding assistants write the plumbing, generative tools fill the asset gaps, and one person can ship in months instead of years. The catch is brutally honest: a community census of 53,597 Steam releases (mid-2023 to mid-2026, run by Pax Dei co-founder Sulka Haro) found AI-tagged games make up about a third of new releases but capture only 10-27% of revenue, and roughly 94% of all Steam money goes to the top 1% of games. The realistic play is a small game with one clear hook, priced $4.99-$9.99, not a genre bet.

AI localization is the proven winner. The same census split AI-tagged games by outcome, and the pattern is unambiguous: among AI games that failed, 72% used AI for imagery - the use players punish hardest. Among commercially successful AI-tagged games, localization shows up at 18% versus 6% for failures, and voice work at 24% versus 8%. Translating your store page and in-game text into 5-8 languages costs near-zero with AI and multiplies the audience without touching the visuals players judge. AI art as the selling point fails; AI under the hood sells.

Sell software, not just games. Steam is not only games. Wallpaper Engine and similar utilities sell in the millions, and the store's software section accepts tools - an AI-powered desktop app can live there with the same 70% split and the same buyer density. The store page requirements apply the same way.

Run a catalog, not a lottery ticket. Polish publisher PlayWay (Car Mechanic Simulator) runs 474 Steam entries: they open dozens of cheap store pages, watch which ones build wishlists, and only finish the winners - 16 releases in a single month, per VG Times. You can copy the mechanism at small scale: the $100 fee applies per app, but a Coming Soon page is cheap, and demos or DLC attached to an existing app cost no extra fee. Wishlists are Steam's vote; let them vote before you build the whole game.

The AI Disclosure Rules

The system has two generations. January 2024 brought the original rule: declare AI-generated content on the store page or the game gets rejected. January 16, 2026 rewrote it: AI coding assistants and other behind-the-scenes productivity tools no longer need disclosure at all - Valve's own form states that efficiency gains are not the focus of the section. What remains is what players see or hear:

  • Pre-generated: art, textures, audio, voice, writing, translation, and the marketing material on your store page. Declared and shown to buyers.
  • Live-generated: anything the game creates while running, like LLM NPC dialogue. You must describe the guardrails; live-generated adult sexual content is banned outright; players can report illegal output through the Steam overlay.
  • Licensing: all AI-generated content must be commercially licensed. The disclosure carries legal weight for you as the developer.

The label is binary - a game with one upscaled texture carries the same line as one fully assembled by a generator - and there is no filter that hides AI-tagged games from players. Scale check: roughly 13 AI-tagged games shipped per month before disclosure existed; it is now around 530 per month, and 49.9% of October 2026 releases carried the label (community analysis of about 63,600 games). Between 8,000 and 10,000 Steam titles disclose AI depending on whose count you trust.

The Honest Cons

  • The flood is real and it costs everyone. The submission surge pushed reviews from 3-5 business days to as long as 12, and Valve tightened the checklist - your build must run through Steam for at least 5 minutes before you can submit it for review. Steam Next Fest was described by Kotaku as "flooded" with AI releases.
  • The AI label has stigma attached. Community analysis links AI disclosure to up to 53% worse sales performance. The causation runs both ways - small, low-effort games disclose more - but buyers do read that section, and AI imagery as the headline feature is the single most punished pattern.
  • Money concentrates at the top. 94% of Steam revenue goes to the top 1% of games. Steam gives you a shelf and discovery machinery; it does not give you an audience.
  • The $100 is per app and returns only if the game sells. A $4.99 game that moves 100 copies never clears the $1,000 line - the fee is gone. Free games pay it too.
  • Wishlists are your job. Launch visibility is driven mostly by wishlist momentum you build yourself through a Coming Soon page, a demo, and Steam Next Fest. The $100 buys the slot, not the marketing.

Bottom Line

Steam Direct is the cheapest distribution to real PC buyers that has ever existed, and the AI disclosure system - which every other store avoided - is a moat for honest builders: you can prove what your AI did and did not do. The math to respect: one clear hook, $4.99-$9.99, AI spent on localization and voice instead of screenshots, a Coming Soon page the moment you have anything to show, and a demo ready for Next Fest. The $100 fee is the smallest number in this business. The wishlist number is the one that decides whether you ever see it back.

FAQ

Do I get the $100 Steam Direct fee back?

Yes, as a credit in a payout once that app passes $1,000 adjusted gross revenue. It is per app - demos and DLC attached to the same app need no extra fee, but standalone apps each cost $100. If the game never sells, the fee is lost, and free games pay it too.

Can I publish a game made with generative AI?

Yes - since January 2024, with a public disclosure on the store page. Since the January 2026 rewrite, coding assistants and dev-side tools need no disclosure; player-facing AI content (art, voice, music, text, translation) does. Live-generated AI must have described guardrails, adult sexual content is banned, and all AI content must be commercially licensed. One practical note from the community census: successful AI-tagged games put AI into localization and voice, while games that lead with AI-generated imagery fail at far higher rates.

When does money actually reach my bank account?

Valve pays monthly, roughly 30 days after the month ends, once your balance is over $100, by bank transfer in local currency where supported. Non-US developers should file the W-8BEN treaty form in Steamworks before the first payout - without it, up to 30% is withheld for the IRS; with it, most countries drop to 0-10%.
