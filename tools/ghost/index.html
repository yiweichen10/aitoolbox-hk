WHAT WORKS
  • ✓  0% platform fee on subscription revenue is permanent and written into every paid plan - at $2,000/mo MRR that saves roughly $2,400/yr versus Substack's 10% cut
  • ✓  Open source under MIT and run by a non-profit foundation: you can export everything, self-host the same software, and the product cannot be sold or shut down under you
  • ✓  Newsletters, memberships, tiers and Stripe subscriptions are native - no plugin stack to maintain like WordPress membership setups
  • ✓  Migration tooling from Substack and WordPress is built in, so moving posts, subscribers and paid members is a documented process, not a rebuild
  • ✓  Email sending is unlimited on every plan, with no per-email surcharge as your list grows
LIMITATIONS
  • —  There is no free plan, and the $15 Starter tier cannot take payments at all - a paid newsletter starts at $29/mo (Publisher, yearly billing)
  • —  Publisher pricing scales with total members (free ones included), not just paid ones: 25,000 members pushes you to $141/mo even if only 50 of them pay
  • —  Self-hosting is real sysadmin work: server, MySQL, NGINX, updates, and Mailgun is the only supported newsletter provider if you go that route
  • —  No plugin marketplace - anything beyond the built-ins (Zapier, webhooks, Admin API) is DIY integration work
  • —  It is a publishing platform only: no real ecommerce, no courses, no community feeds - you will still need separate tools for those

Publisher and Business prices scale with member count: Publisher runs $63/mo at 5,000 members, $88 at 10,000, $141 at 25,000, $208 at 50,000, $274 at 100,000 (all yearly billing). Subdirectory hosting (yoursite.com/blog) is a $50/mo add-on. Ghost(Pro) has no free plan. The software itself is free under MIT and can be self-hosted; self-hosted newsletters require Mailgun as the email provider. Stripe processing fees (2.9% + 30c in the US) always apply on top and go to Stripe, not Ghost.

I ran a paid newsletter on Substack for two years before I did the math on the 10% fee. When the list is small, ten percent feels like nothing. The month my publication crossed $2,000 in monthly recurring revenue, that "nothing" became $240 a month - $2,880 a year - for hosting I could replace with a $29 subscription. I moved the publication to Ghost the following month, and the migration is the reason this review exists.

Ghost is open-source publishing software with newsletters, memberships, and paid subscriptions built into the core. Not bolted on through plugins, not rented from an ad network. The two structural complaints you hear about newsletter platforms - Substack taking 10% of everything forever, and Medium paying out of a black-box member pool - are both solved here, because Ghost holds none of your money. Stripe does, under your own account, and Ghost never touches it.

The honest headline before anything else: Ghost is not free, and the cheap tier cannot charge for content. If you want a $0 newsletter, use beehiiv's free plan or Substack. Ghost is the tool you pick when the newsletter has become a business and the platform cut has become a line item worth killing.

The Money Angle

Four ways people actually earn with Ghost, in the order most publications grow:

  • Paid newsletter subscriptions. The core loop. You set tiers (monthly, annual, founding member), gate posts by tier, and Stripe collects the money into your account. On $1,000/mo of subscriptions, Ghost's platform fee is $0 and your cost is the Publisher plan ($348/yr) plus Stripe processing (~$382/yr at 2.9% + 30c). The same revenue on Substack costs $1,200/yr in platform fees plus Stripe. The gap widens every year you grow.
  • Free list, then sell something else. Plenty of publications run Ghost with a free list and monetize through products, sponsorships or client work, using the member system to segment who sees what. The list is exportable at any time, which is the real asset - no platform can hold it hostage.
  • Premium community content. Membership tiers work for niche research digests, deal lists, and industry briefings where 200 readers paying $10/mo beats 20,000 free readers. Ghost has no community features (no Discord-style feeds), so pair it with Slack or Discord if discussion matters - the tier gate still runs through Ghost.
  • Agency and client newsletters. Because the software is open source and themes are code, a common side income is setting up and maintaining Ghost publications for local businesses and creators, charging a build fee plus monthly maintenance. The Admin API on Publisher makes recurring work scriptable.

If you monetize attention at all - ads, affiliates, sponsors - the ownership matters more than the 0% fee. Sponsors pay for your list, and a list you can export is worth more to them and to you.

Three details people miss:

  • Publisher pricing scales with total members, including free ones. The official slider shows $63/mo at 5,000 members, $88 at 10,000, $141 at 25,000, $208 at 50,000. A big free list is an asset, but it is a paid asset on Ghost.
  • Stripe fees never disappear. 2.9% + 30c per US card charge (more on international cards). Ghost's 0% refers to its own cut only.
  • Self-hosting is genuinely viable. The same MIT-licensed software runs on a $6-12/mo server. The real cost is your time: setup, updates, security, and Mailgun - the only newsletter provider Ghost officially supports when self-hosted. Break-even versus Publisher happens somewhere around 1,500-2,000 members once you value your hours at anything above zero.

The Honest Cons

  • The Starter tier is a trap for monetizers. $15/mo sounds like the entry point, but it cannot accept payments, cannot use custom themes, and caps at 1,000 members. Anyone planning to earn is really shopping for Publisher at $29/mo.
  • No plugins, no app store. WordPress comparisons end here. Integrations run through Zapier, webhooks, or code. If your workflow needs a specific SaaS connection and Zapier does not cover it, you are writing it yourself.
  • Publishing only. No courses, no community, no ecommerce, no digital downloads. Creators who sell products end up pairing Ghost with Gumroad or Ko-fi and maintaining two stacks.
  • You inherit all the boring jobs. Custom domain, deliverability, GDPR footnotes, refunds - all yours. Substack handles much of that for its 10%; on Ghost the 0% fee comes with operator duties.
  • Design work is on you. Themes are Handlebars templates. The default Caspar/Casper look is clean but generic; standing out means buying a theme or editing code. Capterra reviewers (4.7/5, small sample of 53) repeat this exact complaint.

Bottom Line

Ghost is the "graduate from the 10% platforms" move, and it does that job better than anything else because the 0% fee, the open source license, and the non-profit foundation remove the three usual platform risks at once: the cut, the lock-in, and the acquisition. The price of all that is that Ghost is software, not a service: you pay real money from month one and inherit the operator role. If your paid publication makes more than about $300-400/mo, the math already favors Ghost. Below that, a free tier elsewhere is the honest answer - and migrating later is a documented weekend, not a rebuild.

Questions we get asked

Is Ghost actually free?

The software is free and open source, but running it is not. Ghost(Pro) managed hosting starts at $15/mo (Starter) and a monetizable publication needs Publisher at $29/mo yearly. Self-hosting drops the platform bill to a $6-12/mo server but adds your own setup and maintenance time, plus Mailgun for newsletter delivery.

How much does Ghost take from my subscription revenue?

0%. Every paid plan above Starter charges no platform fee on member revenue. You connect your own Stripe account and pay only Stripe's processing fees (2.9% + 30c per US card charge). Compare Substack, which keeps 10% of everything your paid list earns, forever.

Can I move an existing paid newsletter from Substack?

Yes. Ghost has an official importer that moves posts and subscribers, and paid members can be transferred by having them re-enter card details or by migrating subscriptions through Stripe. Budget a weekend for a mid-size publication and expect to rebuild your theme, since design does not carry over.
