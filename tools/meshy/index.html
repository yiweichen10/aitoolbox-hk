Meshy turns text prompts and reference images into textured 3D models, and it is the tool that moved AI 3D from demo footage into production pipelines. Image-to-3D, auto-rigging and a 500+ animation library mean an indie developer can go from concept art to a walking, animated character in an afternoon - work that either costs $50-200 per model from a freelance artist or weeks of learning Blender.

Where the money angle sits

Three commercial uses dominate. Indie game development is the biggest: Steam's indie economics reward shipping fast, and Meshy covers the prop-and-environment layer - crates, weapons, foliage, furniture - at near-zero marginal cost, while Steam Direct handles the storefront. Asset packs are the second: artists generate themed sets (dungeon pieces, sci-fi props, stylized animals), polish them, and sell repeatedly on CG marketplaces - Creative Market is one channel - where a pack priced $20-60 sells without further marginal cost. The third is client visualization: product mockups, real-estate staging previews and AR prototypes generated from client photos through multi-view image-to-3D, a service agencies now bill for that did not exist three years ago.

The honest drawbacks

Generated meshes usually need a retopology pass before close-up production use - clean topology is not what AI generation produces, and 'game-ready' is an overstatement for hero assets. The free tier's CC BY 4.0 license is a real constraint: commercial products built on free-tier assets inherit a public, attribution-required license, which most studios refuse. Credits burn on failed attempts at the same rate as successes, so experimental prompting has a real cost, and the API keeps assets only 3 days on Pro. Complex organic characters and precision hard-surface parts remain outside its range - Spline covers interactive 3D design on the web side, and Luma AI takes the capture-to-3D route with real-world scenes. Bottom line

For indie teams and asset sellers, Meshy is the best value-per-credit in AI 3D right now, and the credit meter is the only ceiling that matters. For production-critical characters, budget for a human retopo pass regardless of which tool you pick. Current plans sit on the Meshy pricing page.

Questions we get asked

Can I sell 3D assets generated with Meshy?

Yes on paid tiers - Pro and above grant a private commercial license, so you own the output and can sell it. The free tier is the trap: its CC BY 4.0 license makes generated assets public and requires attribution, which rules out most commercial packs. Marketplace policies add a second layer - Unity, Unreal and CG stores each set their own AI-content rules, so check the destination's terms before uploading. The workflow options are compared in our 3D modeling tools guide, and Creative Market is one sales channel.

Is Meshy actually game-ready for Unity or Unreal?

For props, environments and background assets, close to it - generate, light cleanup, import through the Unity or Unreal plugin, done. For hero characters or anything seen at close range, no: generated meshes need a retopology pass and manual UV work to hit production standards. The honest framing is 'prototype-fast, hero-assets-with-help'. Indie teams get the most value by generating the 80% of assets nobody looks at closely and hand-modeling the ones players touch.

Meshy vs hiring a 3D artist - when does each win?

Budget decides. At $20/mo for up to 100 assets against $50-200 per outsourced model, Meshy wins on volume and speed - filling a game world, testing art direction, producing client mockups. A human artist wins on anything signature: main characters, brand mascots, assets that appear throughout the product. The working pattern in small studios is hybrid - generate bases with Meshy, pay an artist for the retopo and polish on hero assets. Alternatives like Spline and Luma AI cover different 3D needs, per our comparison.
