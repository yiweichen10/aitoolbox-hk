WooCommerce is the storefront you get when you stop renting commerce software and start owning it. It is a free, open-source plugin that turns WordPress into a full store platform - products, carts, checkout, orders, taxes, shipping - and it is estimated to run roughly a quarter of all online stores on the internet. Automattic maintains it, but nobody owns your store but you: the code is on your server, the data is in your database, and the platform takes exactly 0% of anything you sell.

That ownership pitch matters more in 2026 than it used to. Hosted platforms have spent years quietly converting 'simple pricing' into revenue: Shopify app stacks that grow $100/month at a time, BigCommerce's gateway fee for anyone bringing their own processor, GMV thresholds that auto-upgrade your plan mid-holiday-season. BigCommerce is more honest about this than most, but the model itself - rent the platform, pay by the feature - is the industry default. WooCommerce is the standing exception: costs track your infrastructure, not your revenue.

Pricing: the real math

The official pricing page is refreshingly blunt about what free means. Core plugin: free, forever, no subscription. Hosting: $25-350/month for most stores, your choice of provider - and this is the line item that decides everything, because a slow host kills a Woo store faster than any missing feature. Extensions: $29-299/year each when you need them. Payments: any gateway, no penalty, and you negotiate your own processing rates as volume grows.

WooCommerce now publishes real total-cost-of-ownership examples through an agency partner. The European luxury brand doing $2M/year: about $89,500 in ongoing annual costs, of which payment fees were $50,000 and hosting $300. Platform cost as a share of revenue: 4.48%. A hosted competitor at that revenue would take a revenue-share slice plus app fees on top. The catch, which the same page's fine print admits: initial development on that store was $70,000. Free software does not mean free stores.

The AI gap, honestly

Here is where the review gets uncomfortable. Compared with Shopify Magic - native product descriptions, campaign generation, recommendations - WooCommerce's native AI is nearly nonexistent. You get Jetpack AI Assistant for descriptions and content, AI extensions in the marketplace, and whatever your hosting provider bundles. It works, but it is assembly required, and assembled stacks need maintenance. If AI features out of the box are your deciding factor, Shopify or BigCommerce serve them better today. If AI is a tool you plug into a stack you control - your own data, your own models, your own prompt pipelines - WooCommerce is actually the better substrate, because nothing in a hosted SaaS lets you run custom automation against your own storefront data the way a self-hosted store does.

Where it hurts

The maintenance burden is the real price tag. Updates, backups, staging, security, plugin compatibility - someone owns all of it, and that someone is you. The classic failure mode is not a hack; it is two well-reviewed extensions that break checkout together after a routine update on the night of your biggest campaign. Managed WooCommerce hosting ($30-100/month) removes most of the operational load and is worth every dollar for anyone without a developer on call. The other friction: default checkout, tax and shipping flows in most regions need real setup or paid extensions that hosted platforms configure for you at signup.

How people actually make money with it

WooCommerce has the strongest services economy in the category, precisely because stores need humans. Client builds run $1,500-10,000; maintenance retainers at $100-500/month per store turn each launch into recurring revenue; plugin-conflict rescues and speed optimization are productized services that sell on urgency. On the product side, the extension marketplace is a distribution channel: publish a $79/year extension, and a few thousand installs is a real business. Migrations complete the loop - merchants fleeing app fees on hosted platforms pay $2,000-6,000 to come over, and they arrive pre-qualified as people who hate platform taxes.

Verdict

WooCommerce is the right answer when ownership, cost-at-scale or customization is the priority, and the wrong one when time-to-launch and hands-off operations are. It pairs naturally with content-heavy WordPress sites and with tools like Klaviyo for email and 10Web for AI-assisted WordPress management. The platform's honest one-liner: you trade monthly convenience for long-term control, and the people who come out ahead are the ones who actually invest in maintaining what they own.

👍 Pros

  • Zero platform tax, permanently: no revenue share, no GMV-gated plan auto-upgrades, no transaction fee for using 'the wrong' gateway - costs track your infrastructure, not your sales volume
  • You own the whole stack: code, database, hosting, checkout - no replatform panic when a CEO wants a custom quote flow, a wholesale price list or a data residency requirement a SaaS platform cannot sign off on
  • WordPress content and commerce in one system - blogs, landing pages, SEO plugins and product catalogs share one URL structure and one admin, which content-led stores genuinely profit from
  • Any gateway, no penalty: run Stripe and PayPal and a local BNPL side by side, negotiate your own processing rates, customize checkout without buying a higher plan first
  • The extension economy pays both ways: buy what you need at $29-299/year, or develop and sell your own extension into a marketplace with millions of installed stores

👎 Cons

  • Native AI is close to absent: product descriptions and recommendations come from Jetpack AI Assistant or third-party extensions, and nothing matches Shopify Magic's integration depth - you assemble the AI stack yourself and maintain it
  • You are now the sysadmin: updates, backups, staging, PCI scoping, plugin compatibility and security response all land on you or whoever you pay - a SaaS store owner never thinks about any of this
  • Plugin conflicts are the recurring tax of the platform: two extensions that each work fine alone can break checkout together after a routine update, and debugging falls on you, not a support queue
  • Total cost is opaque up front: the plugin is free but real stores pay hosting plus extensions plus a developer, and the difference between a $25/mo host and a $150/mo one is usually the difference between a store that converts and one that times out
  • Checkout and cart UX needs work by default - shipping calculations, taxes and payment flows in most regions require setup time or paid extensions that Shopify and BigCommerce ship configured out of the box

❓ FAQ

Is WooCommerce really free if hosting costs $25-350/month?

The software is genuinely free - no subscription, no revenue share, no feature tiers. What you pay for is infrastructure and capability: hosting ($25-350/month for most stores per WooCommerce's own pricing page), extensions at $29-299/year each when you need them, and payment processing at whatever rate you negotiate. A lean store runs on a $30/mo host and three free extensions. The honest comparison with Shopify is total cost at your volume: under roughly $50K/year in sales, hosted platforms are often cheaper in hours spent; past that, or for stores needing custom logic, WooCommerce's fixed costs usually win because nothing scales with your revenue except your own hosting bill.

How do I make money with WooCommerce without running my own store?

Four proven lanes. First, client work: WooCommerce builds go for $1,500-10,000 depending on customization, and maintenance retainers of $100-500/month per store stack into real recurring revenue - every store you build is a monthly line item. Second, migrations: merchants priced out of Shopify app fees or BigCommerce gateway fees move to Woo, and those projects bill $2,000-6,000. Third, product: publish your own extension at $29-299/year and the math compounds fast across even a few thousand installs. Fourth, rescue work: stores broken by plugin conflicts or slow hosting pay well for emergency fixes, and speed optimization for underperforming Woo stores is a repeatable productized service.

WooCommerce or Shopify in 2026?

Decide by ownership needs and budget shape, not feature checklists. Shopify wins on time-to-launch, native AI (Magic handles descriptions, campaigns and recommendations), support and not having to think about hosting. WooCommerce wins on cost at scale, customization freedom, content-commerce integration and payment flexibility - and it is the only sane answer when requirements include data residency, complex B2B quoting, unusual product logic or deep WordPress content operations. A practical rule: if the store is the whole business and the team is non-technical, Shopify. If the store lives inside a content-heavy site, needs custom flows, or the margins are thin enough that revenue-share and app fees matter, WooCommerce.

How bad is the maintenance burden in practice?

It is real and it is the platform's main cost, hiding under the 'free' label. Budget for: WordPress and plugin updates (weekly), a staging environment, automated offsite backups, a security plugin or firewall, and someone competent on call when an update breaks checkout the night of a campaign. Stores that skip this get hacked or break eventually - WooCommerce's flexibility is rented with maintenance time. The pragmatic setup: managed WooCommerce hosting (which handles performance and backups for $30-100/mo), a shortlist of well-maintained extensions instead of a pile of cheap ones, and a monthly update-and-test routine you actually follow.
