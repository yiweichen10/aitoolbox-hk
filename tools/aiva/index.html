What AIVA Actually Is (From a Freelance Composer's Desk)

I score videos, games, and ads for a living. For years that meant either paying session musicians I could not afford or grinding out tracks in a DAW at 2 a.m. AIVA changed the math. It is an AI composition engine: you pick a style, set a mood and a tempo, and it writes a full instrumental track with real structure - intro, build, drop, outro. Not a four-bar loop, an actual song.

I use it as a first-draft machine. AIVA lays the skeleton in about a minute, I pull the MIDI into Logic, swap a few instruments, fix the transition, and we ship. What pulls me back, though, is the money side: on the Pro plan the copyright is yours. You can sell the track, license it to a brand, register it with a performing-rights organization. That is rare in AI music - most tools keep a claim on everything you make and limit where you can post it.

The honest framing: AIVA is a composer's assistant, not a karaoke machine. It writes instrumental music you can own and edit. If you want a song with words, this is the wrong tool - that is Suno's lane. But for anyone selling background music, scores, or soundtracks, AIVA is the closest thing to a print button for income.

The Features That Pay the Bills

250+ Styles With Real Song Structure

AIVA does not just hum a mood. It outputs a arranged piece across cinematic, classical, electronic, jazz, pop, lo-fi, and more, with sections that actually resolve. For a client brief like "tense 90-second build for a product reveal," that saves me from staring at a blank timeline. The styles are broad enough that I rarely start from scratch.

MIDI and Stems Export (The Pro Superpower)

This is the feature that separates AIVA from Suno and Udio. AIVA hands you MIDI files and, on Pro, multi-track stems (individual instrument tracks). I drop the MIDI into Logic, move the string section up an octave, kill the hi-hat that fights the voiceover, and export a mix that sounds hand-made. Suno and Udio give you a finished audio render with no editable source - you get what you get. For paid work, editable source is the whole game.

Influence Upload (Match a Reference)

You can upload a reference track or MIDI and AIVA will write something in that vein. When a client sends "make it sound like our old intro," I feed the old intro in and get a close starting point instead of guessing. It is not a clone, but it kills the back-and-forth on style.

Copyright Ownership on Pro

On the Pro plan, full copyright transfers to you - unrestricted commercial use, sync licensing, broadcast, the works. Free and Standard keep the copyright with AIVA. This single detail decides whether you can sell the output, so I treat Pro as the entry ticket to any paid use.

Built-In Editor

AIVA has a DAW-style editor where you can tweak notes, instruments, and arrangement before exporting. It will not replace Logic for a final mix, but for a quick fix - pull the brass down, extend the outro - it keeps you in one tab instead of round-tripping to desktop software.

No Vocals - and That Is the Line

AIVA writes instrumental music only. No lyrics, no sung melody, no "song with words." If a client wants a vocal track, AIVA cannot do it; that is Suno or Udio. Knowing this upfront saves you from selling something you cannot deliver.

How to Make Money With AIVA

AIVA's income comes from ownership. Because Pro gives you copyright, everything you generate can be sold, licensed, or wrapped into a product. Here is what has worked for me and composers I know.

Path 1: Stock Music Packs (Mostly Passive)

Generate a batch of tracks in a theme - "30 Cinematic Intros," "20 Lo-fi Study Beats" - and sell the pack on Gumroad, your own site, or a marketplace. Because Pro transfers copyright, you own every track and can license it forever. Price a 20-track pack at $29-$49. One pack sold ten times covers a year of Pro. The work front-loads: a weekend of generation and light MIDI cleanup, then it earns while you sleep. I have a "corporate calm" pack that still sells two or three copies a month, eighteen months after I built it.

Path 2: Custom Scoring for YouTubers and Filmmakers

Indie creators and small production houses need original scores but cannot hire a composer at union rates. You take their brief, generate three options in AIVA, refine the chosen one in your DAW, and deliver stems. Charge $75-$300 per project based on length and revision rounds. AIVA turns a job that used to take me two days into an afternoon, so I can take four to six scoring jobs a month at $150 average - roughly $600-$900 against a $36 software bill. The client gets an original, owned-by-them track; you get repeat business because every video needs new music.

Path 3: Game and App Soundtracks

Indie game devs and app makers need loops and stingers but live on tight budgets. You build a cohesive soundtrack - menu theme, battle loop, win sting - and license it per title. Charge $200-$1,000 per game depending on scope, or sell loop packs on asset stores for $15-$65 a pop (passive). AIVA's style control keeps the whole OST consistent, which is the hard part of game audio. One dev I work with buys a $400 pack per project and comes back for the sequel.

Path 4: Ad and Brand Music for Agencies

Local agencies and in-house marketing teams burn through music for ads, explainers, and social cuts. You offer a "custom score per deliverable" service: $100-$500 per ad depending on length and whether they want stems for their editor. Because AIVA generates fast, you can turn a brief around in a day and take three to five brand clients a month. At $300 average that is $900-$1,500 monthly for work that fits between other jobs. The copyright clarity is your sales pitch - they own it, no royalty surprises later.

Path 5: Podcast and Course Audio Beds

Podcasters and course creators need intro music, outro beds, and segment transitions but hate the licensing maze. You set them up with a small library of owned tracks: $30-$150 per episode package, or a $50-$200 monthly retainer for a show that refreshes weekly. This is pure margin - generate once on Pro, reuse across the season, charge for the curation and the clean handoff. Five shows at $100 a month is $500 for maybe two hours of work.

The Unit Economics

Blended across those paths at a realistic five active clients plus one stock pack: AIVA Pro at about $36 a month (billed annually). Revenue lands around $1,500-$3,500 a month once the catalog and clients compound. Margin sits near 95% because the subscription is your only real cost and your time is already yours. The constraint is lead flow and how fast you can clean a track in your DAW - not the tool. AIVA pays for itself the moment one client pays more than the monthly fee, which happens on the first job.

What AIVA Is Bad At (The Honest List)

1. Instrumental only - no vocals, no lyrics. If a client asks for a "song with words," AIVA cannot do it. It writes music, not singing. Send that work to Suno or Udio, or pair AIVA's bed with a vocalist. Assuming otherwise will cost you a client.

2. Copyright stays with AIVA on Free and Standard. This trips up beginners. On the free and Standard plans, AIVA owns the copyright and only grants limited posting rights. You cannot sell those tracks or register them. Full ownership requires the Pro plan. I have seen people generate a "client track" on Standard, then discover they cannot hand over rights - embarrassing and fixable only by re-generating on Pro.

3. Standard's monetization is locked to four platforms. The Standard plan lets you earn only on YouTube, Twitch, TikTok, and Instagram. A podcast, a client commercial, a game, or a film needs the Pro plan's unrestricted license. If your buyer is anyone but a social creator, Standard will not cover you.

4. Pro is pricey if you do not need it. At about $36 a month billed annually (more on monthly), Pro is steep for someone who just wants background music for their own videos. The 300-download monthly cap is far more than most solo users burn. If you only score your own content, Standard might be enough - but you still will not own the copyright.

5. Polished results need manual MIDI editing. AIVA gets you to 70-80%, not broadcast-ready. The transitions can be stiff, the mix unbalances, and a "hero" moment rarely lands without a human pass in a DAW. Treat it as a draft machine, not a finish line. Anyone promising "studio-quality from a prompt" is selling something AIVA is not yet.

6. The free plan is too tight to judge the tool. Three downloads a month, up to three minutes, non-commercial, credit required. You can poke the interface, not evaluate the breadth across 250 styles. Budget for at least one month of Standard before you decide if it fits your work.

Getting Started Without Wasting Money

  • Start free, but know the wall. Make three tracks, see the limits, and accept you cannot sell anything on the free plan. Use it to learn the style picker, not to build a catalog.
  • Master the MIDI export before anything else. The moment you can pull AIVA's MIDI into your DAW and fix it fast, the tool pays. Practice on one track until your cleanup pass is under twenty minutes. Editable source is the whole reason to pick AIVA over Suno.
  • Buy Pro only when you sell. Free and Standard keep copyright with AIVA, so any paid client work needs Pro. Land a scoring job or a pack sale first, then upgrade that week. Do not promise ownership on a lower tier.
  • Build packs, not one-off tracks. A themed 20-track pack on Gumroad earns long after you make it; a single custom track pays once. Spend your generation budget on batches you can productize, then take custom jobs with the leftover time.
  • Use influence upload to match client briefs. When a client sends a reference, feed it in. You skip the style debate and start from "close," which cuts revision rounds and keeps the job profitable.
  • Keep a real composer for hero moments. AIVA handles beds, loops, and most score work. For the one cue that carries the whole film, bring in a human or do the deep edit yourself. Knowing which is which is what keeps clients happy.
