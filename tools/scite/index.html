Most "AI research tools" are search engines with a chatbot bolted on. Scite does something different and, in my experience, more useful: it has analyzed over 1.6 billion citation statements across 300 million+ scholarly sources and labeled each one as supporting, contrasting, or just mentioning the paper it cites. That last part matters. When I find a paper claiming X, the real question is not "does this paper exist" - it is "what happened when other researchers tried the same thing." Scite answers that directly, with the actual citing sentences on screen.

Two features carry the tool:

Smart Citations. Open any paper and you see how every later citation treated it. A cancer-nutrition study with 400 citations looks a lot less solid when 60 of those citations are explicitly contrasting. I have caught disputed-adjacent claims this way before they made it into client deliverables.

Reference Check. Upload your own draft (PDF or manuscript) and Scite checks your reference list against the citation record - flagging which of your sources were disputed by later work. It is a pre-submission smoke detector. I run it on every evidence-heavy article before delivery.

The Assistant is a chat layer on top: it answers questions with inline citations and shows the citing context for each. There is also an official MCP server, which means Claude or any MCP client can pull Scite's citation data straight into your workflow - that is how I run claim-checks without leaving my editor.

The Money Angle: Sell the Truth-Check

Nobody hires you to find papers - Semantic Scholar is free. What people pay for is *certainty*: knowing a claim survives contact with the citation record. Scite is the cheapest tool on the market that delivers that, and here is how I have turned it into invoices:

  • Evidence dossiers ($500-$3,000 per review). Health-tech startups, supplement brands, and law firms regularly need to know whether the studies behind their marketing claims hold up. A dossier tracing 20-30 key claims through supporting and contrasting citations takes me 4-8 hours with Reference Check and the Assistant, and prices at $1,000-$2,000 for mid-market clients. One dossier covers a year of Pro.
  • Cite-checking retainers for content agencies ($200-$500/mo per client). Content teams get burned by citing studies that later work contradicted. I run their draft claims through Scite monthly and deliver a "disputed/solid/retracted" report. It is 2-3 hours of work per client once the MCP workflow is set up.
  • Systematic-review support for academics ($1,500-$5,000 per project). Screening citation context is the miserable part of a review. Scite does not automate the judgment, but it cuts the time per record dramatically.
  • Build on the Pro API. The API plus the $60 credit is the quiet sleeper here - a citation-check bot or due-diligence tool wired into a client's workflow is a sellable product, not just a service.

The honest framing: Scite is not a fat affiliate program. You make money by selling the output - verified claims - and the tool makes that output 5-10x faster to produce.

How I Actually Use It Week to Week

  • Claim triage. Paste the 15-20 load-bearing claims from a client brief into the Assistant, pull the citing context for each, and sort them into solid/disputed/mixed. About 90 minutes for a full brief.
  • Competitor teardown. When a rival product cites "17 studies," I check how many of those are actually contrasting. Sales teams love that slide.
  • MCP workflow. In Claude, I ask for a claim-check and the Scite MCP server returns classified citations inline. The 2,500 Pro credits cover roughly my monthly volume; Basic's 250 credits are enough for light personal use only.
  • Collection building. I keep a 1,000-paper collection per client vertical with citation alerts on. When a key source gets disputed, the client hears it from me first.

Where It Falls Short (Honest)

  • The labels are machine-made. Supporting/contrasting classification misfires on methods-heavy citations - a paper can be labeled "contrasting" for disagreeing about methodology while confirming the core finding. Scite shows the actual citing sentence, which is exactly why you can overrule a bad label in seconds, but never trust a label you have not eyeballed.
  • No full-text rescue. Scite finds citation context for paywalled papers but usually cannot show the paper itself. Without institutional access you will hit walls.
  • Coverage skews biomed/CS. Deep in medicine and tech, noticeably thinner in the humanities and in very recent preprints.
  • The trial-to-paid cliff. Seven days is enough to learn the tool, not enough to finish a real review project. Plan the trial around a small pilot.
  • Collection caps. 1,000 papers on Basic sounds generous until a systematic review shows up.

Scite vs Elicit vs Consensus

I run all three and they do not overlap the way people assume. Elicit screens and extracts across a defined paper set - it is the assembly line for a 300-abstract review (my full Elicit review). Consensus is the fast "what do papers generally say" aggregator - great for a first answer, risky as the final word (review here). Scite is the auditor: it does not find answers, it checks whether the answers survive. If you buy one for client work, it is Scite; if you buy two, add Elicit. ❓ FAQ

Can I actually make money with Scite if I am not an academic?

Yes - you sell certainty, not paper-finding. Evidence dossiers for health-tech and supplement brands ($1,000-$2,000 for a 20-30 claim trace), cite-checking retainers for content agencies ($200-$500/mo per client for a monthly disputed/solid/retracted report), and systematic-review support for academic teams ($1,500-$5,000 per project). One dossier covers a year of the Basic plan. What you cannot do is resell the tool itself - there is no meaningful affiliate program.

How accurate are the supporting/contrasting labels?

Good enough to save hours, not good enough to trust blind. Machine classification misfires most on methods citations - a paper can be flagged 'contrasting' because it disputed the methodology while confirming the finding. Scite's saving grace is transparency: every label comes with the actual citing sentence on screen, so you verify in seconds. Treat labels as triage, and eyeball anything you put in a paid deliverable.

Scite or Elicit - which one should I pay for?

They solve different bottlenecks. Elicit screens and extracts data across a defined set of papers - the assembly line for a structured review. Scite checks how later work cites specific claims - the audit step. If your bottleneck is screening 300 abstracts, buy Elicit; if it is verifying whether key claims survived replication and critique, buy Scite. Plenty of working researchers run both; at $12/mo, Scite is the cheaper second subscription.

Is there a free plan or a student discount?

No free tier - just a 7-day Pro trial, then Basic at $12/mo billed yearly (~$20 monthly). Students and university staff should check institutional access first; many universities buy Enterprise with unlimited pooled users, which makes your personal card pointless. If your library does not subscribe, the yearly Basic at $144 is the entry price, and there is no real way around it.
