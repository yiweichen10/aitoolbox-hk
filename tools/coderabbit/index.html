CodeRabbit is an AI code reviewer that sits in your pull requests - it reviews every diff, learns from your feedback, and applies one-click fixes. Where GitHub Copilot writes code and Claude Code drives the terminal, CodeRabbit is the second pair of eyes that never gets tired of reading diffs. For freelancers and agencies, that second pair of eyes is billable time recovered.

What the plans actually cost

Verified on coderabbit.ai/pricing, 2026-11-27. Essentials costs $24/user/mo with 1 connected repo, 5 reviews per hour and 5 MCP connections. Team at $48/user/mo raises that to 5 repos and 8 reviews/hour, adding unit-test generation, triage queues (prioritized PR reviews with Slack integration), change-stack chat and post-merge actions. Advanced at $72/user/mo goes to 10 repos, 10 reviews/hour, plus the security layer: risk-prioritized findings, blast-radius audits, continuous security monitoring and security-based merge blocking. Enterprise is custom - 20 repos, SSO, RBAC, admin API, self-hosted deployment and support SLAs. Every paid plan carries a 14-day free trial; the current page lists no permanent free plan and no annual discount. Two usage-based add-ons live outside the plans: Deep Scan (whole-codebase security sweep) and Agent (Slack-driven task automation).

Where the money angle sits

The math that matters is review hours. An agency with five developers on Team spends $240/mo and typically recovers 3-6 hours per developer per week of review back-and-forth - at contract rates the subscription pays for itself in the first week. Solo contractors use Essentials to ship client work with a reviewer attached, and sell "senior review included" as a differentiator. Deep Scan opens a productized audit: a fixed-price security sweep of an acquired or inherited codebase, billed to the client. Open-source maintainers get review coverage without burnout - note the current page no longer lists a permanent OSS free tier, so verify your repo's status before planning around it. Pairs well with Aider and Cline workflows where AI-generated code needs a review layer, and our coding assistants comparison maps the full stack.

The honest drawbacks

Per-user pricing stacks fast: five developers on Team is $240/mo, ten on Advanced is $720/mo. Repo allowances are tight - Essentials covers one repo, which rules out contractors juggling several client codebases unless they upgrade. Hourly review caps (5-12 per hour by tier) throttle busy merge days, and overages are usage-priced. No annual discount is published, and the free-for-open-source arrangement that made CodeRabbit popular is not on the current page. Review quality on niche frameworks and internal DSLs produces noise - the agent learns from feedback, but week one on an unfamiliar codebase needs patience. When the reviewed code is itself AI-generated at volume, Cursor or Windsurf workflows surface issues CodeRabbit then re-flags - some overlap is inherent.

Bottom line

CodeRabbit is the easiest yes in AI dev tooling for teams whose bottleneck is review latency: the 14-day trial settles the question on your real codebase. Solo devs with one repo should weigh $24/mo against their actual review backlog. Current plans sit on the CodeRabbit pricing page.

Questions we get asked

Is there a free version of CodeRabbit?

The current pricing page lists no permanent free plan - paid tiers start at $24/user/mo, all with a 14-day free trial. CodeRabbit was known for a free open-source tier earlier; if you maintain an OSS repo, verify your repo's status on the site before planning around it.

How is CodeRabbit different from Copilot or Cursor?

They cover different halves of the loop. GitHub Copilot and Cursor write code; CodeRabbit reviews what gets written - in the PR, with learning from your feedback and security checks. Most teams run both: a writing assistant plus a review layer.

What is CodeRabbit Deep Scan?

A separate usage-based product that sweeps an entire codebase like a security engineer - findings ranked by exploitability with fix guidance. It fits fixed-price audit engagements on inherited or acquired codebases, complementing the per-PR review.
