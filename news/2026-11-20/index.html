Gemini for Mac Adds Computer Control

Google shipped computer use for Gemini on Mac: multi-step tasks across desktop apps, local files and the web, running in background windows without occupying the cursor. Rolling out first to US subscribers.

WhyComputer use just moved from developer APIs into consumer apps, with Google monetizing the lane Anthropic created through subscription tiers. Google's own risk disclosures are notable: accessibility and screen-recording permissions mean it can read logged-in sessions and verification codes - and Apple tightened macOS permissions three days earlier. Trust competition is now running ahead of capability competition.

Perplexity Brain Writes Its Own Personal Skills

The Perplexity Brain agent automatically writes and updates personal skills from conversation history. In an 8-week randomized test, 72.9% of users gained new skills and tool-call failures dropped 11% at unchanged cost.

WhySkills are memory externalized without weight changes: Brain finds repeated patterns in conversation traces, only writes when evidence is sufficient, caps 10 skills per user, and shared-skill changes require replay tests plus human approval. An 8-week controlled trial showing an 11% failure-rate drop at flat cost is the first quantified evidence for the 'gets better the more you use it' claim.

Google Opens Genie 4 World Model API

Google opened Genie 4 to developers: text- or image-generated interactive 720p 24fps worlds with 48kHz stereo audio and keyboard control, at about $6.05/minute with 120-second session limits.

Stanford Benchmark Tracks AI-Written Court Filings

Stanford's PhantomDocket benchmark calibrates detectors using an AI-hallucination corpus: in 2026, 38% of Santa Clara County complaints showed AI traces, with self-represented filers rising from 19% to 72%.

WhyThe same 2026 filings, run through the same detector at different settings, produced detection rates from 10% to 98% - general-purpose detectors cannot be taken at face value on specialized text like legal documents. The team's approach of calibrating against confirmed-hallucination cases is pragmatic; the findings support estimating population prevalence, not catching individuals. What courts actually need is a disclosure system.
