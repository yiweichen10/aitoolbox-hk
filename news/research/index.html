Meta details LLM pipeline behind Facebook Your Algorithm ranking

Meta detailed the engineering behind Your Algorithm, which lets Facebook users type interests for Feed and Reels. Each typed interest drives four retrieval sources, and a fine-tuned LLM reranker outputs only items to remove, which Meta says beat a general-purpose model on speed and reliability. The system passed no user metrics.

WhyThe interesting choice is the reranker's output: returning a short removal list is cheaper than scoring every candidate, a pragmatic cost trade-off that keeps an LLM inside a latency-sensitive ranking loop. For users, the appeal is explicit control over an opaque feed, but Meta published no engagement or accuracy figures, so the design is verifiable while its effect on recommendations is not.

DeepMind agent teams cut quantum algorithm costs using Lean proofs

DeepMind used teams of Gemini 4 agents to optimize quantum algorithms, proving each circuit correct with the Lean theorem prover and open-sourcing the library. For inertial confinement fusion, agents cut computing cost 11.8-fold and memory from 5,650 to 4,338 error-corrected qubits. They also found a 290-fold speedup for arcsin and 2.5-fold for Cytochrome P450 simulations.

WhyThe method's value is less any single speedup than the proof step: Lean verification makes agent-proposed circuits checkable when hardware cannot test them. That turns AI search into something quantum researchers can trust without waiting for fault-tolerant machines. The 290-fold arcsin gain is an arithmetic routine, not an end-to-end application, so the leap to drug design still depends on resources no early error-corrected computer can supply.

Anthropic Mathematician Claims Claude Proof of the Riemann Hypothesis

Mathematician Alpöge says the claimed proof also covers the generalized Riemann hypothesis, published as 14 Lean packages spanning over 250,000 printed pages. Hours later, Stanford released a competing human-only draft by mathematician Masullo.

WhyLean formalization makes a quarter-million-page proof machine-checkable line by line for the first time - but whether the problem statement was correctly translated into code still relies on expert spot-checks, and that will remain the long-running controversy around AI mathematical proofs. Meanwhile, the human draft had its questionable points found by other AI-assisted reviewers within eight hours. The two routes checking each other is more notable than who proved it first.

MiniMax MIM Achieves First Practical Breaks on Multiple NIST Candidates

MiniMax's MIM delivered the first practical cryptanalysis breaks on several NIST candidate algorithms, solving 55 previously unsolved McEliece challenge instances - up to a 43,000x speedup over prior records.

WhyCryptanalysis is an ideal agent testbed: binary success, attack scripts either run or fail, no room for hallucination. MIM releases traces and proofs-of-concept but not the model itself - internal research models remain the moat. No need to panic about deployed standards, but NIST candidates falling one after another shows the safety margin is thinner than assumed.

METR Update: AI Task Time Horizon Doubles Every 3.6 Months

Covering 15 models through GPT-6 Astra and Opus 5.5, the merged TH1.1+TH2 suite shows a 3.61-month doubling cycle; the TH2 verifiable subset doubles in just 1.65 months.

WhyThe doubling cycle is an extrapolation, not a promise: 3.61 months is based on TH tasks, and the 1.65-month verifiable subset may be inflated by task-mix effects. What deserves attention is not 'when does it hit 200 hours' but that models still trail human experts in domains with high lifecycle costs and weak fast-feedback loops - the framing is shifting from raw capability to cost and judgment.

Stanford Benchmark Tracks AI-Written Court Filings

Stanford's PhantomDocket benchmark calibrates detectors using an AI-hallucination corpus: in 2026, 38% of Santa Clara County complaints showed AI traces, with self-represented filers rising from 19% to 72%.

WhyThe same 2026 filings, run through the same detector at different settings, produced detection rates from 10% to 98% - general-purpose detectors cannot be taken at face value on specialized text like legal documents. The team's approach of calibrating against confirmed-hallucination cases is pragmatic; the findings support estimating population prevalence, not catching individuals. What courts actually need is a disclosure system.
